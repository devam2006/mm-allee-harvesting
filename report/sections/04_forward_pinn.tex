\section{Forward PINNs}

\paragraph{Scope and labels.} All trajectories are \emph{synthetic} and dimensionless
($x=N/K$, $\tau=rt$); no species-specific claim is made. The question is how well a small
neural network that is given only the ODE and the initial condition reproduces the
population, compared with a standard solver.

\subsection{Method}
For Model A, $\dot x=G(x)-q$, and Model B, $\dot x=G(x)-ex$, with $G(x)=x(1-x)(x/a-1)$ and
$\dot x=dx/d\tau$, write $\dot x=f(x)$. The network is a fully connected network with layer sizes
$(1,32,32,1)$ in float64 (JAX) and the \emph{swish} activation $\sigma(z)=z/(1+e^{-z})$ (chosen among tanh, swish and sigmoid, see below).
The initial condition is enforced \emph{exactly} by the hard embedding
\begin{equation}
\hat x(\tau)=x_0+s\,N(s;\theta),\qquad s=\tau/T,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the network and $T$ a fixed constant, so $\hat x(0)=x_0$ for any weights $\theta$ and no
initial-condition loss is needed. Automatic differentiation with respect to the physical time $\tau$ applies the chain
rule $d\hat x/d\tau=T^{-1}\,d\hat x/ds$ (including the product rule on $s\,N(s)$). The residual and the only loss are
\begin{equation}
R(\tau)=\frac{d\hat x}{d\tau}-f(\hat x),\qquad
\mathcal L(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n R(\tau_i)^2 ,
\end{equation}
with $n=300$ collocation points, a fixed uniform grid on $[0,T]$ including both endpoints. The time input is only scaled by the fixed constant $T$
(no centring or nonlinear input map).
Training uses 3500 iterations: Adam (1000 iterations, rate $10^{-2}$, about $2/7$ of the budget) to move into a good region,
then L-BFGS-B (2500 iterations, SciPy, using the exact JAX gradient) to converge. No solver
solution is used as a label. For Model A, $T$ is the extinction time of the reference solution
(a domain endpoint only), so the original residual is never evaluated on a post-extinction zero tail.
For Model B, $T=10$.

\subsection{Evaluation}
Predictions are compared on a separate dense grid of 1001 points with DOP853
($\mathrm{rtol}=10^{-12}$, $\mathrm{atol}=10^{-14}$), not used in training. We report RMSE, MAE, global relative
$L^2$ error $\|\hat x-x_{\rm ref}\|_2/\|x_{\rm ref}\|_2$ (always given in scientific notation, never as a percentage),
the maximum absolute error, the maximum absolute error over the times where $x_{\rm ref}<0.05$ (the near-zero error; undefined if the reference never goes below $0.05$), the number of negative predicted samples, and wall-clock time.
A prediction containing NaN or infinity is never scored: it is labelled \emph{invalid} and has no error score (it can never count as accurate), and a run that raises an exception is recorded as \emph{failed} without stopping the sweep.
A run is labelled \emph{inaccurate} if its relative $L^2$ error exceeds $5\times10^{-2}$, a threshold fixed
before the final run. The classical baseline is the project RK45 wrapper, scored on the same grid.

\subsection{Activation selection}
With the design above (seeds 0, 1, 42; six cases; 54 runs) tanh, swish and sigmoid were compared. The selection rule was fixed beforehand: the activation with the
most accurate runs out of 18, ties broken by the smaller geometric-mean relative $L^2$ error.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
Activation & accurate runs (of 18) & geometric-mean rel.\ $L^2$ & median rel.\ $L^2$\\\hline
swish & 17 & $2.3\times10^{-5}$ & $2.2\times10^{-5}$\\
tanh & 11 & $2.9\times10^{-4}$ & $7.5\times10^{-5}$\\
sigmoid & 10 & $1.4\times10^{-3}$ & $4.0\times10^{-3}$\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Swish was accurate in A regular and B regular for all three seeds, where tanh managed 0 of 3 and 1 of 3 and sigmoid 0 of 3 and 1 of 3.
\emph{Caveat:} the winner was selected on the same seeds as the final sweep, so its reported accuracy is optimistic; the held-out confirmation below (seeds 11, 22, 33, not used for the choice) found 18 of 18 runs accurate.
We do not claim to know why swish works better here.

\subsection{Results (swish, 300 points, seeds 0, 1, 42; 18 PINN runs)}
Relative $L^2$ errors by case:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
Case & seed 0 & seed 1 & seed 42\\\hline
A regular & $1.8\times10^{-5}$ & $3.6\times10^{-5}$ & $2.1\times10^{-5}$\\
A near-fold & $\mathbf{5.8\times10^{-2}}$ & $2.9\times10^{-3}$ & $4.2\times10^{-3}$\\
A subthreshold extinction & $1.2\times10^{-6}$ & $2.9\times10^{-7}$ & $9.5\times10^{-7}$\\
B regular & $5.0\times10^{-5}$ & $2.4\times10^{-5}$ & $1.3\times10^{-6}$\\
B near-fold & $8.4\times10^{-7}$ & $5.1\times10^{-6}$ & $8.0\times10^{-8}$\\
B subthreshold decay & $1.6\times10^{-4}$ & $3.5\times10^{-5}$ & $4.2\times10^{-4}$\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Seventeen runs met the accuracy threshold and one did not (bold); none was invalid, failed or produced a non-finite loss. In every run L-BFGS stopped at its 2500-iteration limit (SciPy exit ``iteration limit reached'', not an abnormal line-search exit), so the iteration budget is binding; longer runs were not tried.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A near-fold} (collapse after a bottleneck) is the hardest case. In this run one of three seeds (seed 0, $5.8\times10^{-2}$) just exceeds the threshold, but a different seed failed in independent reruns on other machines and library versions (see below), so the robust statement is that \emph{about one in three seeds misses the threshold}, not that a particular seed does.
\item \textbf{Near-zero error} (largest absolute error where $x_{\rm ref}<0.05$): A extinction $5.1\times10^{-8}$--$3.1\times10^{-7}$, B decay $1.3\times10^{-6}$--$1.8\times10^{-5}$, A near-fold $6.2\times10^{-3}$--$1.3\times10^{-1}$ (largest for the seed that missed the threshold).
\item \textbf{Negative samples.} Some runs predict slightly negative populations near the zero limit: B subthreshold decay seeds 0 and 42 (83 and 114 of 1001 samples, largest error $\le2\times10^{-5}$), A near-fold seed 42 (2), A extinction seeds 0 and 42 (1 each).
\item \textbf{Cost.} Every PINN run took $4.8$--$6.0$ s (including compilation); RK45 took about $10^{-2}$ s with relative $L^2$ error of order $10^{-10}$--$10^{-9}$.
On this scalar ODE the conventional solver is faster and more accurate by many orders of magnitude, so the value of the PINN here is as a method to be
understood and as the engine for the inverse problems (Task 5), not as a better solver.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Held-out confirmation and reproducibility}
\paragraph{Held-out confirmation.} The frozen design (swish, 300 points, 3500 iterations) was run unchanged on seeds 11, 22, 33, which were not used to choose swish (selection used seeds 0, 1, 42). All 18 runs were accurate; relative $L^2$ errors by case: A regular $8.1\times10^{-7}$--$4.6\times10^{-5}$, A near-fold $1.3\times10^{-4}$--$3.9\times10^{-2}$, A extinction $2.9\times10^{-7}$--$1.0\times10^{-6}$, B regular $1.2\times10^{-6}$--$9.6\times10^{-6}$, B near-fold $4.8\times10^{-7}$--$7.6\times10^{-7}$, B decay $4.9\times10^{-5}$--$1.9\times10^{-4}$ (\texttt{artifacts/tables/wp04\_confirmation\_metrics.csv}). The A near-fold seed 22 error ($3.9\times10^{-2}$) is close to the threshold, so 18 of 18 should not be read as a guarantee.
\paragraph{Independent reruns.} Reviewers reran the frozen design on Linux with Python 3.11 and newer library versions (JAX 0.10.2) and reported 17 of 18 on seeds 0, 1, 42 but with a \emph{different} failing run (A near-fold seed 1, $8.3\times10^{-2}$), 16 of 18 in a third rerun, and 18 of 18 on seeds 11, 22, 33. Per-seed results are therefore sensitive to platform and library version even with fixed seeds, while the counts are reasonably stable. The exact versions behind every reported table (JAX, jaxlib, NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib, Python, operating system) are recorded in the run manifests and in \texttt{artifacts/manifests/wp04\_forward\_requirements.txt} and \texttt{wp04\_confirmation\_requirements.txt}.

\subsection{Diagnostic: why the regular cases failed with tanh and 100 points}
This is an \emph{exploratory} experiment (script \texttt{experiments/wp04/diagnose.py}, hypotheses written before running) performed on the earlier design (tanh, 100 uniform points, seeds 11, 22, 33), where
both regular cases failed for most seeds despite a tiny training loss. It trains A regular and B regular with 10 seeds under five collocation designs and, besides the error
against the reference, measures the ODE residual $|R|$ on a dense grid of 4001 times, \emph{between} the training points.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
Accurate runs out of 10 (tanh) & U100 & U200 & U400 & P100 & U100\_long\\\hline
A regular & 3 & 3 & 5 & 10 & 3\\
B regular & 2 & 3 & 4 & 10 & 2\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
U$n$: $n$ uniform points; P100: 100 points $\tau_i=T(i/99)^2$ clustered near $\tau=0$; U100\_long: U100 with 10000 instead of 2500 L-BFGS iterations.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mechanism.} Every accurate run (45 of 100) has $\max|R|\le1.2\times10^{-3}$ on the dense grid and every failed run (55) has
$\max|R|\ge8.4\times10^{-1}$, while 45 of the 55 failures had $\max|R|<10^{-1}$ at the training points. The loss at the training points cannot reveal
these failures; a residual check on a denser grid separates all 100 runs.
\item \textbf{Where they end.} Failures settle on spurious equilibria of the ODE: the unstable root (22 runs), zero (6), the unphysical negative root of the Model A polynomial
(2) or elsewhere; 10 runs had not converged (all Model A, uniform grids).
\item \textbf{Resolution near $\tau=0$ matters, not the point count.} Denser uniform grids help only modestly (U400: 5 and 4 of 10 accurate), but the same 100 points clustered
near $\tau=0$ gave 10 of 10 in both cases (median relative $L^2$ $6.5\times10^{-6}$ for A and $5.3\times10^{-6}$ for B).
\item \textbf{More L-BFGS iterations do not help} (U100\_long: 3 and 2 of 10): stalled runs reached other spurious solutions.
\end{itemize}
The final design (swish, 300 uniform points) was chosen separately from this diagnostic; tanh with 300 points still failed A regular (0 of 3 seeds) and B regular (1 of 3).
\emph{Caveats:} two cases only; the clustered grid was chosen after seeing the failures and has not been run in a frozen sweep for all six cases; 10 seeds cannot exclude a failure
rate up to about $25$--$30\%$; outcomes are sensitive to round-off (a grid differing by one rounding unit changed one failing seed's error from $7.6\times10^{-1}$ to $9.8\times10^{-1}$).

\subsection{Limitations}
One parameter set ($a=0.2$) per regime; one hyperparameter configuration apart from the activation, which was selected on the same three seeds that are reported (and then confirmed on three others);
three seeds only, and failures in other designs were strongly seed dependent; CPU timings from one machine (see manifest). For Model A the horizon $T$ is the extinction time computed by the reference solver, so the forward PINN does \emph{not} predict the extinction time itself: it is supplied, and only the path up to it is fitted. The reason swish outperforms tanh and sigmoid is not understood.
Early-clustered collocation and causal time-marching were not combined with swish. The previous sweep (tanh, 100 points, seeds 11, 22, 33; 12 of 18 accurate) is kept for provenance in
\texttt{artifacts/tables/}\allowbreak\texttt{wp04\_forward\_metrics\_n100\_tanh\_seeds11-22-33.csv}.

    --output results/wp04 --artifacts artifacts
\end{verbatim}
