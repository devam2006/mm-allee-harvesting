\section{Model B, comparison and harvesting history}
\label{sec:wp02}
\subsection{Proportional harvesting and steady populations}
Model B removes biomass at rate $hN$, so the realised catch decreases when the
population decreases. For example, $h=0.05\,\mathrm{year}^{-1}$ gives a catch of
$5$ tonnes/year at $N=100$ tonnes and $1$ tonne/year at $N=20$ tonnes.
These are illustrative quantities, not observations of a particular stock.
With the scaling of Section~1, the equation is
\[
 f_B(x)=\frac{dx}{d\tau}=x\left[(1-x)(x/a-1)-e\right],\qquad e=h/r.
\]
Zero is always an equilibrium. Nonzero equilibria satisfy
$x^2-(1+a)x+a(1+e)=0$, giving
\[
 x_\pm=\frac{1+a\pm\sqrt{(1-a)^2-4ae}}{2},\qquad
 e_c=\frac{(1-a)^2}{4a},\qquad h_c=re_c.
\]
For $0\leq e<e_c$, the factorisation
$f_B(x)=-x(x-x_-)(x-x_+)/a$ gives the phase-line signs
$-,+,-$ on $(0,x_-)$, $(x_-,x_+)$ and $(x_+,\infty)$.
Thus $x_-$ is unstable and separates the initial populations tending to zero
from those tending to $x_+$. The upper state is stable.
At zero, $f'_B(0)=-1-e<0$, so zero attracts from the physical side.
At $e=e_c$, $x_-=x_+=(1+a)/2$ and
$f_B(x)=-x[x-(1+a)/2]^2/a$: the root attracts from above and repels from below.
It is semistable, not stable merely because its derivative vanishes.
For $e>e_c$ only zero remains. At $e=0$ the three equilibria are $0,a,1$.
The quantitative basin and extinction analysis belongs to WP03; these signs
provide the equilibrium interface used here, without a second collapse solver.

The implementation returns records with population, derivative, multiplicity,
one-sided signs, stability and residual. The control field is $e$, not WP01's
$q$, to preserve the distinction between effort and yield. The smaller root
uses $a(1+e)/x_+$ to avoid subtractive cancellation. Only exact equality to the
computed $e_c$ is labelled critical; distinct representable controls retain
their topology. If the roots cannot be separated at floating-point precision,
the code raises an error rather than silently merging them.

\subsection{Two explicit comparison protocols}
$H$ has units biomass/time whereas $h$ has units inverse time, so equality of
their numerical values is not a fair comparison. We use the same initial
population, $r$, $K$ and (for A/B) $A$, under two distinct protocols:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Equal initial realised catch: $H=hN_{\rm ref}$ with
$N_{\rm ref}=N_0$. We set $H=\alpha H_c^{A}$, then obtain $h$ from the matching
condition. The logistic baseline also uses this same $H$.
\item Equal fraction of each threshold:
$H_A/H_c^{A}=h/h_c=H_L/H_c^{L}=\alpha$, where $H_c^{L}=rK/4$.
This matches distance to the respective critical control, not initial catch.
\end{enumerate}
The recorded grid uses $r=0.5\,\mathrm{year}^{-1}$, $K=1000$ tonnes,
$A=200$ tonnes. Initial populations satisfy $N_0/K\in\{0.15,0.4,0.9\}$ and
$\alpha\in\{0.5,1.2\}$. The observation horizon is $\tau=30$ (60 years).
The CSV separates the theoretical long-term outcome, determined from the
phase line, from events actually observed during this horizon. A blank basin
boundary means no positive equilibrium, not a zero threshold.

For $N_0=900$ tonnes and $\alpha=0.5$, the measured comparison is:

The logistic baseline collapses in the first protocol because the common catch
exceeds its own critical value, 125 tonnes/year. This does not show that Allee
growth is universally safer: equal $r,K$ does not equalise production curves,
and the additional Allee factor can increase growth at high densities.
For the first protocol, the mean catches over 60 years are 131.28, 134.00 and
103.35 tonnes/year for A, B and logistic, respectively. The latter includes
harvest shutdown after extinction. B's total catch is integrated numerically;
A/logistic catch equals $H$ times the pre-extinction duration. Mean catch always
divides by the complete observation horizon. No universal risk ranking is claimed.

\subsection{Harvesting history, collapse and restocking}
We use a declared staircase schedule with 12 upward increments to $1.3$ times
the critical control, a 12-unit peak hold and 12 downward increments back to
zero. The initial zero-control interval and each non-peak ramp interval have
dwell $d=1$ or $3$ in dimensionless time. After the downward ramp there is a
15-unit zero-control recovery hold and a final 20-unit hold. Starting at $x=1$,
we carry each segment's terminal population into the next segment. Two rows at
the same boundary time represent the left and right limits, not extra elapsed
time. There is no hidden reset at the turning point.

The unassisted and intervention runs share the entire schedule. The latter
adds exactly $0.6$ to $x$ at the beginning of the final hold, at $\tau=51$ or
$99$. Since the control is then zero, this takes the population above the
unstable threshold $a=0.2$. This addition is an intervention, not spontaneous
recovery or a change in the differential equation between interventions.
A stops at the first downward zero event and thereafter has zero effective
harvest and an absorbing zero population until any declared restocking.
B is never reset to zero when small. From a positive start its exact solution
does not hit zero in finite time. Its reported smallness time is the first
sample at $x\leq10^{-6}$, dependent on sample spacing, not an exact event time.

At both speeds the unassisted population remains below the Allee threshold
when harvesting ends, while the restocked run returns to $x\simeq1$.
The faster ramp crosses its equilibrium critical level before A reaches zero;
this finite passage delay is not a changed equilibrium threshold. Dwell-time
changes alter transient times and total exposure, so these experiments are not
an isolated causal estimate of ramp speed. They demonstrate history dependence
and a recovery barrier, not a closed spontaneous hysteresis loop.

\subsection{Validation, reproducibility and limitations}
The runner uses the shared NumPy models and event-aware RK45 solver.
Default tolerances are relative $10^{-9}$, absolute $10^{-11}$ and maximum
step $0.1$. Each restocking history is repeated with $10^{-11}$, $10^{-13}$
and $0.05$, respectively. Across the four cases the largest sampled population
difference is below $2\times10^{-9}$ and A event-time difference below
$2\times10^{-9}$, inside the predeclared $2\times10^{-7}$ acceptance limits.
These are absolute dimensionless error limits, far below the $0.2$ recovery
threshold; they do not resolve relative accuracy of tiny B tails below the
absolute tolerance. No quantitative ecological significance is attached to
those tails. The B catch quadrature is separately checked by doubling the
sample density. High-precision expanded polynomials, independent numerical
maximisation, direct vector-field signs and perturbed trajectories test the
analytical results rather than relying on a large test count alone.

Reproduce from the repository root:
\begin{verbatim}
python experiments/wp02/run.py --config configs/wp02.json
    --output results/wp02
\end{verbatim}
Use one command line (the line break above is for typesetting). A 120-second CPU budget is checked between
bounded solves; this is not a hard operating-system timeout. The manifest
records configuration, actual tolerances, versions, hardware, source hashes,
commit and dirty status, elapsed time and output checksums. There is no noise,
random seed, empirical dataset or neural training in this package.
Comparison support is restricted to $0.05\leq a\leq0.95$ pending WP01's
near-boundary numerical fixes; the recorded experiment uses $a=0.2$ far from
those problematic regimes. WP03's threshold helpers are not yet present in
this checkout; A reuses WP01's helper and B uses its discriminant derivation.
Optional relaxation-time experiments are deferred. Final integration,
independent human reproduction, four approvals and presentation rehearsal
remain separate requirements. Codex assisted derivation, implementation,
testing and drafting; human understanding is not certified by these checks.
