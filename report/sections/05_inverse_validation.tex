\section{Inverse PINNs, Identifiability, and Held-Out Validation}
\label{sec:wp05}

This section estimates unknown parameters of the strong-Allee harvesting models from
discrete population observations. It addresses two questions:
(i) whether the physical parameters are uniquely determined by ideal trajectories
(structural identifiability), and (ii) how accurately conventional ODE least squares
and an inverse physics-informed neural network (PINN) recover parameters and predict
a held-out time window.

\subsection{Model B structural nonidentifiability and counterexample}
\label{sec:identifiability}

Under Model B, harvesting is proportional to population, at a constant effort
level $h$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dN}{dt}
=
rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)\left(\frac{N}{A}-1\right)-hN.
\label{eq:modelB_inverse}
\end{equation}
Expanding the cubic gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{dN}{dt}
=
-(r+h)N
+r\left(\frac{1}{A}+\frac{1}{K}\right)N^2
-\frac{r}{AK}N^3
=
c_1N+c_2N^2+c_3N^3,
\label{eq:model_b_coefficients}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
c_1=-(r+h),\qquad
c_2=r\left(\frac{1}{A}+\frac{1}{K}\right)
=\frac{r(A+K)}{AK},\qquad
c_3=-\frac{r}{AK}.
\end{equation}
At a single fixed effort, the trajectory therefore depends on the four physical
parameters $(r,A,K,h)$ only through the three composite coefficients
$(c_1,c_2,c_3)$. The parameters cannot, in general, be uniquely recovered from
such a trajectory: Model B is \emph{structurally nonidentifiable} when all four
parameters are unknown and effort is held fixed.

We observe an explicit counterexample:
\begin{align}
(r,A,K,h)_1&=(1.0,\,0.2,\,1.0,\,0.5),\\
(r,A,K,h)_2&=(1.35,\,0.3,\,0.9,\,0.15).
\end{align}
Both sets produce
\begin{equation}
(c_1,c_2,c_3)=(-1.5,\,6.0,\,-5.0),
\qquad
\dot N=-1.5N+6N^2-5N^3.
\end{equation}
They consequently define the same vector field and the same trajectories from
every common initial population. The numerical demonstration overlays the two
parameter sets for three initial populations, $N(0)=0.4$, $0.7$, and $1.1$;
the corresponding curves coincide (Figure~\ref{fig:wp05_counterexample}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{wp05_counterexample}
\caption{Structural nonidentifiability in Model B. Two distinct parameter sets
produce the same three polynomial coefficients and therefore overlapping
population trajectories from each of the three initial populations.}
\label{fig:wp05_counterexample}
\end{figure}

Using several initial populations at the same effort does not resolve the
ambiguity: all such trajectories are solutions of the same vector field and
do not provide a fourth independent coefficient. An independently known
parameter, such as the carrying capacity $K$, is needed to constrain the
problem. The single-case demonstrations below use Model A and treat $K$ and the
initial population as known; the multi-regime benchmark also includes Model B.

\subsection{Observations, noise generation, and frozen 60/20/20 splits}
\label{sec:inverse_design}

For the single-trajectory demonstrations, the reference is Model A with
\begin{equation}
r=0.7,\qquad K=100,\qquad A=20,\qquad H=10,\qquad N(0)=45.
\end{equation}
The clean trajectory is sampled at 101 equally spaced observation times on
$t\in[0,8]$. Noisy observations are generated by
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{obs}}(t_i)
=
N_{\mathrm{clean}}(t_i)
+\sigma N_{\mathrm{ref}}\epsilon_i,
\qquad
\epsilon_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),
\qquad N_{\mathrm{ref}}=100.
\label{eq:wp05_noise}
\end{equation}
The external reference scale is fixed at $N_{\mathrm{ref}}=100$; thus
$\sigma=0.01$ corresponds to a noise standard deviation of one population
unit. Observations are not silently clipped, including when additive noise
would produce a negative value. The clean and 1\% noisy Model A demonstrations
use noise seed 11.

The 101 observation times are partitioned into contiguous, non-overlapping
time windows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Training:} 61 points, $0\leq t\leq 4.80$ (60\%). These are
  the only observations used in the data-fit objective.
  \item \textbf{Validation:} 20 points, $4.88\leq t\leq 6.40$ (20\%). These
  observations are excluded from the training gradients. The clean
  demonstration and the frozen benchmark disable validation-based checkpoint
  selection; the separate noisy demonstration enables it.
  \item \textbf{Held-out test:} 20 points, $6.48\leq t\leq 8.00$ (20\%).
  These observations remain untouched until the post-training evaluation.
\end{itemize}
The split counts and endpoints are shown in the figure~\ref{fig:wp05_splits} below.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{wp05_demo_observations_1pct}
\caption{Model A observation design for the 1\% noisy demonstration. The clean
reference and noisy observations are shown with the contiguous training,
validation, and held-out test windows.}
\label{fig:wp05_splits}
\end{figure}

For these demonstrations, the carrying capacity and initial population are
treated as exact known conditions ($K=100$ and $N(0)=45$). The fitted physical
parameters are therefore $(r,A,H)$. The exact initial population is supplied
to both methods, including for noisy observations, so both fits have a
noise-free initial-condition anchor. This is an idealisation relative to a
setting in which the initial population must itself be estimated.

\subsection{Baseline inverse PINN on clean data}
\label{sec:clean}

The first inverse-PINN demonstration uses the noise-free Model A observations
($\sigma=0$). The true parameter values are
$(r,A,H)=(0.70,20.0,10.0)$, and the common initial guesses are
$(1.0,30.0,5.0)$. The network has two hidden layers of 32 units with
$\tanh$ activation, and the initial condition is imposed exactly as
$x(0)=x_0$.

The physical parameters are represented through smooth transformations that
keep the estimates in their admissible ranges:
\begin{equation}
r=\operatorname{softplus}(\widetilde r)>0,\qquad
A=K\,\operatorname{sigmoid}(\widetilde A)\in(0,K),\qquad
H=\operatorname{softplus}(\widetilde H)\geq 0.
\label{eq:wp05_parameter_transforms}
\end{equation}
The objective combines data fidelity on the training observations
($t\leq4.8$) with the Model A physics residual at 300 collocation points.
The data and physics weights are
$\lambda_{\mathrm{data}}=1.0$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{phys}}=0.5$.
The network learning rate is $10^{-3}$ and the physical-parameter learning
rate is $5\times10^{-3}$. Training uses up to 500 Adam epochs followed by
at most 3000 L-BFGS iterations.
The run uses seed 1011.

The trainer returned status \texttt{converged} after 12.4 seconds, while
L-BFGS itself terminated at its iteration limit rather than at a tolerance.
The final estimates are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{True value} & \textbf{PINN estimate} & \textbf{Relative error}\\
\midrule
$r$ & 0.7000 & 0.7002 & 0.03\%\\
$A$ & 20.0000 & 20.0032 & 0.02\%\\
$H$ & 10.0000 & 10.0025 & 0.03\%\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Thus, for this well-specified, noise-free single-case demonstration, the
inverse PINN recovers all three parameters to within $0.03\%$. The notebook
also records the parameter estimates throughout both the Adam and L-BFGS
phases (Figure~\ref{fig:wp05_clean_parameter_trajectory}).

\subsection{Parameter estimation under 1\% measurement noise: ODE least squares versus PINN}
\label{sec:noise}

The second live demonstration repeats the Model A fit using the same
observations with $\sigma=0.01$, corresponding to a noise standard deviation
of $1.0$. Both methods use the same training observations, known conditions
($K=100$, $N(0)=45$), and initial parameter guesses
$(r,A,H)=(1.0,30.0,5.0)$.

The conventional \texttt{ODE\_LS} baseline integrates the candidate ODE
trajectory with an adaptive RK45 solver and estimates the three parameters
using SciPy's Trust Region Reflective nonlinear least-squares algorithm.
The inverse PINN jointly optimises the neural-network weights and physical
parameters using the two-phase Adam--L-BFGS procedure. It uses the same
two-hidden-layer, 32-unit, $\tanh$ network and 300 collocation points as the
clean demonstration. For this noisy run, validation-based checkpoint
selection is enabled. The network and parameter learning rates are
$10^{-3}$ and $5\times10^{-3}$, respectively; the physics weight is $0.5$;
the Adam budget is up to 1200 epochs; the L-BFGS budget is 3000 iterations. The PINN uses seed 1011.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{Model A parameter estimates for the 1\% noisy demonstration. The
relative error is measured against the true parameter value.}
\label{tab:wp05_noisy_demo}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{True} & \textbf{ODE\_LS estimate} & \textbf{ODE\_LS error} & \textbf{PINN estimate} & \textbf{PINN error}\\
\midrule
$r$ & 0.7000 & 0.5834 & 16.66\% & 0.5804 & 17.08\%\\
$A$ & 20.0000 & 18.1335 & 9.33\% & 18.0672 & 9.66\%\\

\bottomrule

The ODE fit takes 0.426 seconds, compared with 9.95 seconds for
the PINN in this run. The PINN's L-BFGS phase reaches its iteration limit;
validation selection is enabled for this noisy demonstration. The deviation from the true parameters can be understood from the
nature of the inverse problem. Both methods minimize the discrepancy
between the model and a finite set of noisy observations, rather than
directly minimizing the error with respect to the true parameters.
The injected noise has a standard deviation of $1$ population unit,
and only the first 61 of the 101 observations (up to $t=4.8$) are
used for training. Consequently, the particular realization of
measurement noise (seed 11) influences the optimal parameter values:
the noise deviations do not cancel out perfectly over this finite
training sample, and the best-fitting parameters need not coincide
with the values used to generate the data.

This effect is compounded by the interaction between the three
unknown parameters in Model A,
\[
\frac{dN}{dt}
=
rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)
\left(\frac{N}{A}-1\right)-H.
\]
Over the observed interval, changes in these parameters can partially
compensate for one another. In particular, reducing $r$ weakens
population growth, whereas reducing $A$ increases the Allee growth
factor for $N>A$, and reducing $H$ decreases the harvesting pressure.
These competing effects allow different parameter combinations to
produce very similar trajectories. The optimizers therefore settle
on parameter values that provide a good fit to the particular noisy
sample, rather than necessarily recovering the original parameters.

The close agreement between ODE\_LS and the PINN is consistent with
both methods finding a similar compromise under the same observations
and governing equation. The PINN additionally optimizes the neural
network weights alongside the physical parameters, and its L-BFGS
phase reaches the iteration limit rather than satisfying a
convergence tolerance. The held-out RMSE of approximately $1.09$,
which is close to the injected noise standard deviation, further
suggests that both methods capture the underlying population
trajectory to approximately the accuracy permitted by the noisy
measurements. Thus, accurate trajectory prediction does not
necessarily imply equally accurate parameter recovery. These results
are one illustrative noisy fit, not a replacement for the multi-case
benchmark in the next subsection.

\subsection{Final sweep benchmark: 108 fits}
\label{sec:benchmark}

The full benchmark contains 108 fits: 54 inverse-PINN fits and 54
\texttt{ODE\_LS} fits. It spans six dynamical regimes, three noise levels,
and three random seeds:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Model A:} \texttt{a\_regular} (strong-Allee persistence),
  \texttt{a\_near\_fold} (near the saddle-node tipping point), and
  \texttt{a\_extinction} (collapse below the Allee threshold).
  \item \textbf{Model B:} \texttt{b\_regular} (proportional-harvest
  persistence), \texttt{b\_near\_fold} (near threshold), and
  \texttt{b\_decline} (steep transient decline).
  \item \textbf{Noise levels:} $\sigma\in\{0,0.01,0.05\}$, with additive
  Gaussian noise standard deviation $\sigma N_{\mathrm{ref}}$.
  \item \textbf{Seeds:} 11, 22, and 33 for each case and noise level.
\end{itemize}

For clean data ($\sigma=0$), a fit is counted as recovered when
\emph{every} fitted physical parameter is within 5\% relative error of its
true value. The reported median error is the median across the three seeds
of each run's worst relative parameter error. The results are:

inverse problem and optimisation difficulties also play a role.

dynamics rather than measurement noise.

\subsection{Diagnostic analysis: why eight PINN fits were not recovered}
\label{sec:difficult}

Across the 18 clean-data benchmark fits, \texttt{ODE\_LS} meets the 5\%
recovery criterion in all 18, while the PINN meets it in 10. This difference
is not explained by conditioning alone: the PINN recovers all three
\texttt{b\_near\_fold} runs despite their relatively large reported
condition number, but does not recover any of the
\texttt{a\_near\_fold} or \texttt{b\_decline} runs.

\paragraph{Recovered cases.}
The six \texttt{a\_regular} and \texttt{b\_regular} fits are all recovered.
Their trajectories show smooth, monotonic sigmoidal growth, and the
reported Jacobian condition numbers are in the range
$\kappa(J)\in[62,340]$. In these cases the effects of the physical
parameters are sufficiently distinct over the transient phase for the
data and physics objectives to support parameter recovery.

The \texttt{b\_near\_fold} case is recovered in all three seeds despite a
reported condition number of approximately $10^3$. A large condition
number indicates an ill-conditioned sensitivity problem, not mathematical
nonidentifiability by itself. In Model B, proportional harvesting $hN$
scales with population and factors out $N$, while the trajectory in this
case retains dynamic curvature over time. L-BFGS refinement may help
navigate poorly conditioned directions, but the experiment did not measure
an exact Hessian or isolate the cause of the successful recovery.

\paragraph{Unrecovered cases.}
None of the three \texttt{a\_near\_fold} fits meets the recovery criterion.
Near the saddle-node fold, fixed-quota harvesting creates a long dynamic
bottleneck in which $dN/dt\approx0$ (the ``ghost'' of the fold). Parameter
sensitivities can become strongly correlated; in the recorded fits, $r$
and $A$ are nearly collinear, with $r/A$ approximately constant. These fits
can have small trajectory errors while their parameter estimates remain
far from the true values. The bottleneck and correlated sensitivities are
plausible explanations; gradient magnitudes were not measured in this
experiment.

The \texttt{b\_decline} case is not recovered in any of the three seeds,
and \texttt{a\_extinction} is recovered in only one of three. Both regimes
include a steep, rapid transient collapse followed by a long, nearly flat
tail near zero population. Limited information in the transient and the
resolution of the collocation grid are possible contributors, but their
causal effects on these inverse failures were not isolated by the study.

\paragraph{Why the ODE least-squares baseline performs better in this benchmark.}
The two optimisers solve substantially different numerical problems:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Search space.} \texttt{ODE\_LS} estimates three physical
  parameters, $\boldsymbol{\theta}=(r,A,H)$ for Model A (or
  $(r,A,h)$ for Model B). The PINN additionally optimises more than
  1,000 neural-network weights and biases alongside the physical
  parameters.
  \item \textbf{Physics enforcement.} \texttt{ODE\_LS} evaluates each
  candidate parameter set by numerically integrating the ODE with an
  adaptive RK45 solver and declared tolerances. The numerical solver has
  finite error; it is not exact arithmetic. The PINN enforces the ODE
  through a soft residual penalty evaluated at a finite set of
  collocation points.
  \item \textbf{Optimisation landscape.} The baseline uses bounded
  nonlinear least squares with the Trust Region Reflective algorithm.
  These results do not establish that its objective is convex or that it
  has a unique global optimum. The PINN instead optimises a composite
  data-and-physics objective over both network and physical parameters;
  such a problem can contain flat regions, saddle points, and competing
  minima.
  \item \textbf{Sensitivity calculation.} The ODE least-squares
  implementation estimates the residual Jacobian numerically and does
  not supply analytical sensitivities to the optimiser. The PINN uses
  gradients through the neural network and its composite loss.
\end{itemize}

The central distinction is that \textbf{parameter error is not trajectory
error}. A PINN can fit observed points with a trajectory residual around
$10^{-4}$ while its physical parameter estimates remain tens of per cent
or, in some cases, approximately 100\% away from the truth. The ODE
baseline avoids this particular surrogate-fitting route by evaluating the
forward ODE model directly for each candidate physical parameter set.
Possible gradient-stiffness or spectral-bias effects---for example, network
weights fitting observations faster than the physical parameters can
improve the physics residual---are candidate explanations, not mechanisms
demonstrated by the recorded benchmark.

\paragraph{Candidate follow-up methods.}
The following approaches are proposed experiments, not fixes demonstrated
for the eight unrecovered PINN fits:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Adaptive loss weighting:} methods such as SoftAdapt or
  GradNorm could dynamically balance the data and physics objectives
  rather than keeping their weights fixed.
  \item \textbf{Residual-based adaptive refinement (RAR):} collocation
  points could be concentrated in steep collapse and inflection regions
  instead of remaining uniformly distributed in time.
  \item \textbf{Curriculum training or time-domain decomposition:}
  training could progress through successive time windows, resolving the
  early transient before fitting later, flatter dynamics.
\end{enumerate}

The scope of these diagnostics is limited. The initial population is
independently known and exact, including in noisy runs. The ODE observation
model uses zero padding after a candidate trajectory reaches extinction.
The mechanistic explanations for the PINN failures remain hypotheses where
the notebook does not report a direct measurement. The numerical methods used in the comparison are documented in the SciPy
references for \texttt{least\_squares} and L-BFGS-B.

\subsection{Key scientific takeaways}
\label{sec:wp05_takeaways}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Parameter error is not trajectory error.} A PINN can fit
  observations to a trajectory residual of approximately $10^{-4}$ while
  its parameter estimates remain substantially inaccurate in
  ill-conditioned regimes.
  \item \textbf{Classical ODE baselines are strong.} For these
  low-dimensional scalar ODEs, SciPy nonlinear least squares is faster
  and more robust in the clean-data benchmark, recovering the parameters
  across all six regimes.
  \item \textbf{Structural identifiability precedes optimisation.}
  Model B with all four parameters $(r,A,K,h)$ unknown is structurally
  ambiguous at a single fixed effort. No optimisation algorithm can
  resolve this ambiguity without independent information, such as a
  known carrying capacity $K$.
  \item \textbf{The noise floor is a convergence diagnostic, not a
  parameter-accuracy guarantee.} Comparing training RMSE with the noise
  standard deviation,
  $\mathrm{RMSE}/(\sigma N_{\mathrm{ref}})\approx0.9$,
  measures the fit relative to the noise scale. It does not by itself
  establish parameter accuracy or show that all available information
  has been extracted.
\end{enumerate}
