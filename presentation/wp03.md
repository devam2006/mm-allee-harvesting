## Slide 1: where does collapse happen? (0:00–2:15)

Show `wp03_basins.png` and the two threshold formulas. Point to the lower black curve before
describing the upper branch. State that the horizontal axis is a fraction of each model's own
critical control, not equal catches in the two models.

“Our extra Allee factor introduces a minimum viable population. With a fixed catch, the population
changes according to natural production minus harvesting. To find the largest sustainable constant
catch, we maximise the production cubic between A and K. Differentiating gives the upper stationary
point N_star and then H_c=g(N_star). For proportional harvesting, we instead maximise production
per individual, giving h_c. The controls have different units, so comparing their raw numbers is
not meaningful.

“Being below the harvesting cliff is still not sufficient for survival. The smaller positive
equilibrium is an initial-population barrier. Below it, trajectories decline; above it, they tend
to the larger equilibrium. The exact barrier stays put. At the cliff the roots merge and the
equilibrium is semistable: it attracts from above but not from below. That is why we inspect signs
when the derivative is zero.

“For our illustrative parameters, N_star is about 70.55, H_c is about 36.76 population units per
time unit and h_c is 0.56 per time unit. These are model calculations, not estimates for a real species.”

## Slide 2: when does collapse finish? (2:15–4:40)

Show `wp03_extinction.png`, then `wp03_bottleneck.png` if two figures fit legibly.

“With a positive fixed catch, the derivative at zero is minus H. Zero is not an equilibrium of
that original equation. We stop the physical solution at its first zero event. We do not append
a zero tail and pretend it satisfies the same equation. With proportional effort, harvest goes
to zero with the population, so a positive trajectory approaches zero asymptotically.

“To compute the finite time in Model A, we separate variables and integrate one divided by
harvest minus production. Before integrating, we check the basin. A root in the path is a pole:
it means the extinction calculation is not applicable. We record those cases explicitly rather
than treating a missing time as zero.

“We checked the integral against two RK45 tolerances and a separate eighth-order solver using
an independently expanded polynomial. The largest discrepancy in the six configured event cases
is below 4.6 times ten to the minus eight in dimensionless time. Tests also cover different Allee
ratios and exact initial equilibria. Passing tests support those checks; they do not prove every
possible calculation or validate a real ecosystem.

“Just above the cliff, the population can linger near the vanished equilibrium before falling.
Expanding the cubic gives a denominator approximately delta plus beta times the squared distance
from the fold. Integrating gives the inverse-square-root delay shown here. The delay can be long
even though extinction is inevitable. A finite simulation that ends before it collapses must not
be labelled permanent survival.”
